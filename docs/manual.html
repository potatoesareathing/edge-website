Before you startHow to read this manual

You built a working website without knowing what most of it was called. That is a real achievement and also a real problem, because you cannot maintain what you cannot name. This manual gives you the names.

It is written from your actual code — every file opened and read, nothing assumed because it is common. Where something could not be determined from the project files, it is marked Needs verification rather than guessed at.

The three ways to use this

  1. Read it front to back, once. Parts 1–7 build a mental model in order: what the thing is, what the words mean, what happens when someone visits, then each system in depth. Skipping ahead will cost you more time than reading will.
  2. Keep it beside you as a reference. Part 9 is a maintenance inventory — every item you own, how you know it needs attention, and what breaks if you get it wrong. Part 11 is a playbook of step-by-step procedures for the jobs you will actually do.
  3. Reach for Part 12 when something breaks. It teaches you to locate a fault before you try to fix it, which is the single skill that separates people who can maintain software from people who can only ask for help.
A promise about complexity

This manual does not simplify things to make you feel capable. Where your site does something genuinely intricate — the WebGL portal is the clearest case — you get the real explanation, including the mathematics, because a simplified version would leave you unable to debug it. Every technical term is defined the first time it appears, in terms of your code, not in the abstract.

One thing you should know immediately

Your registration form does not work. It looks like it works. A student fills it in, sees a success screen, and their details are written to storage inside their own phone's browser, where you can never reach them. Nothing is sent anywhere.

This is not a bug in the code — the code is waiting for two configuration values you have not supplied yet. Part 6 explains the mechanism and Part 11.14 has the fix. Do not run a club fair before reading it.

Conventions

  • Monospaced text is something literal: a filename, a command, a value in your code.
  • Bordered boxes flag definitions, warnings, and things needing verification.
  • File references look like js/app.js:554 — that is the file, then the line number. Line numbers are accurate at commit 1ca0a30 and will drift as you edit.

Part 1What you actually built

Before any detail, get the shape of the thing in your head. Your site is simpler than you probably fear and more unusual than you probably realise.

1.1  The five honest sentences

  1. Your website is 22 files. One HTML file, one stylesheet, six JavaScript files, four media files, four documents, and a few configuration files. That is all of it.
  2. It has no dependencies. There is no package.json, no node_modules folder, no framework. Nothing was downloaded from anyone to make it run. This is rare and it is a large part of why it will still work in five years.
  3. It has no build step. The files you edit are the exact files a visitor's browser receives. Nothing compiles, bundles, transpiles or minifies. What you see is what ships.
  4. It has no backend of its own. No server of yours runs any code. The site is static files handed out by GitHub. The one piece of server-side work it wants to do — storing registrations — is delegated to an outside service that is not yet connected.
  5. All of its content lives in one file. js/content.js holds every piece of text, every roster, the leaderboard, the events, the FAQ answers. You can run the club's website for a year without opening any other file.

1.3  What your site is not

Half of understanding a system is knowing what it does not contain. People will assume your site has these things because most sites do. It does not:

The thing to take from this

You did not build a small version of a complicated thing. You built a genuinely simple thing that happens to have two sophisticated pieces in it (the WebGL portal and the video system). Simple systems are maintainable by one person. Keep it that way: every dependency you add is a thing that can break while you are not looking.

Part 2The vocabulary

These are the words people will use when they talk to you about your site. Each one is defined here in terms of your actual project, not in the abstract. Read this part once; you will come back to it.

2.1  Client and server

Client

The machine asking for something. On your site, the client is the visitor's phone or laptop, specifically the browser running on it. Everything in the top box of Figure 1 is the client.

Server

A machine that sits waiting for requests and answers them. Your server is a GitHub computer somewhere. When someone types your address, their browser asks that machine for index.html, and it sends the file back.

Your server is a static server: it only hands over files that already exist. It never thinks. It cannot check a password, save a form, or send an email.

The word "server" confuses beginners because it means both the physical machine and the program running on it. When someone says "is your server down?", they mean "is the thing that hands out your files responding?" For you, that is GitHub's problem, not yours.

2.2  Frontend and backend

Frontend

Everything that runs on the client. Your HTML, your CSS, all six JavaScript files, the WebGL portal, the chatbot, the form validation. Your site is 100% frontend.

Backend

Code that runs on a server, where the visitor cannot see or change it. You have no backend of your own. Supabase, once connected, acts as a rented backend: their servers run the database, and you never manage a machine.

This distinction matters enormously for security. Anything in your frontend is readable by anyone — right-click, "View Source", and every line of your JavaScript is there. This is not a flaw; it is how the web works. It is why you must never put a secret password in frontend code, and why the FAQ chatbot is a keyword matcher rather than a real AI model (Part 6.2 explains).

2.3  How a page is actually made

HTML

The structure and content. Your index.html says "there is a header here, a table there, a form below it". It is a skeleton, not a picture.

CSS

The appearance. Your css/styles.css says "headers are white on near-black, 33 pixels tall, in Chakra Petch". Same skeleton, different clothes.

JavaScript

The behaviour. Your six js/ files make things happen: switch tabs, answer questions, validate the form, draw the portal. HTML and CSS are descriptions; JavaScript is instructions.

The DOM (Document Object Model)

When the browser reads your HTML, it builds a live tree of objects in memory. That tree is the DOM. Your JavaScript does not edit index.html — it edits the DOM, and the screen updates to match.

This is why your changes vanish on refresh: the file on disk never changed. It is also why js/content.js exists — to make content changes permanent, they have to live in a file.

2.4  Words about code organisation

Component

A reusable piece of interface. Frameworks make this formal; your site does it with functions that build DOM nodes. postCard() in js/app.js:121 takes one blog entry and returns a finished card. That is a component, even without the label.

State

Information that changes while the page is open and that the interface must reflect. Your site has very little, which is why it is easy to reason about. Examples: which tab is showing (showTab), which join path is selected (let path = "member" in js/join.js:27), how far the portal has opened (progress in js/portal.js:137).

Bugs in complicated apps are usually state bugs: two places disagree about what is true. You avoided most of these by keeping state in one place per system.

Routing

Deciding what to show based on the address. Your site uses hash routing: the part of the URL after the #. Visiting …/#players shows the Players section. showTab() in js/app.js:47 is your entire router — 20 lines.

Middleware

Code that sits between a request arriving and it being answered, doing something to it on the way — checking a login, logging the request. Your site has none, because it has no backend. The word is in this manual only so you are not thrown when someone uses it.

2.5  Words about data moving

API (Application Programming Interface)

An agreed way for one program to ask another for something. Not a thing you can point at — a contract.

Your site uses three: the browser APIs built into every browser (WebGL, Web Audio, localStorage, fetch); the Supabase REST API, which is how your form would save a registration; and Instagram's embed script, which is an API of sorts.

Endpoint

One specific address within an API. Your form's endpoint, once configured, is

https://YOUR-PROJECT.supabase.co/rest/v1/registrations

Built in js/join.js:211 from the URL and table name in content.js. Sending a POST request to that address means "add a row to the registrations table".

Request and response · GET and POST

A request asks; a response answers. GET means "give me something" — every file your site loads is a GET. POST means "here is some data, do something with it" — your form uses POST. The difference matters: GETs can be cached and repeated safely, POSTs change something and should not be repeated blindly.

Status code

A number in every response saying how it went. 200 fine. 404 no such file. 403 forbidden — this is what Netlify returned when its credits ran out. 409 conflict — your form treats this as "this register number already signed up" (js/join.js:226). 500 the server broke.

JSON

A text format for structured data, and the way your form talks to Supabase. It looks like JavaScript objects because it is derived from them:

{"path":"member","full_name":"Asha K","phone":"9876543210"}

2.6  Words about running and shipping

Dependency

Someone else's code your project needs in order to work. Your project has zero. Nothing is installed; nothing can break when its author changes it.

You do use three outside services at runtime (Google Fonts, Instagram, Supabase), which is a different kind of reliance — covered in Part 7.3.

Build process

A program that transforms your source files into the files that actually ship — combining, shrinking, converting newer syntax to older. Your site has none. netlify.toml says command = "", meaning "there is nothing to run".

Deployment

Getting your files from your computer to the machine that serves them. For you, deployment is one command: git push. GitHub Pages notices the new commit and republishes within about a minute.

Hosting

Renting the machine and the address. GitHub Pages hosts you for free, with no bandwidth bill and no credit system — which is exactly why the site moved there.

DNS (Domain Name System)

The phone book of the internet: it turns a name into the numeric address of a machine. You do not use DNS, because you do not own a domain name. GitHub owns github.io and manages its DNS. If you ever buy edgeruas.in, configuring DNS becomes your job — Part 11.17.

CDN (Content Delivery Network)

Copies of your files kept on machines around the world, so a visitor is served from one physically near them. You get one free — GitHub Pages runs your site through a CDN automatically. Google Fonts is also a CDN. Nothing to configure.

Cache

A saved copy kept to avoid fetching again. Caching is the single most common cause of "I fixed it but it still looks broken". Your logo problem was exactly this: the old host was told to keep asset files for 24 hours, so a corrected logo could not reach anyone.

The fix used in your code is cache busting — the ?v=2 on every edge-mark.svg?v=2 in index.html. Change the number and browsers treat it as a different file.

Environment variable

A value supplied to a program from outside its code, so the same code can behave differently in different places — and so secrets stay out of the source.

Your site has none, and cannot have any. There is no build step and no server, so there is nowhere for a value to be injected. This is why your Supabase key would sit in plain sight in content.js, and why the database's security rules — not secrecy — are what protect student data. Part 6.3 covers this properly; it is the most important security idea in your project.

Authentication vs authorisation

Authentication is proving who you are (logging in). Authorisation is what you are allowed to do once known. Your site has no authentication. It does have authorisation, in the database: anonymous visitors may insert a registration and may do nothing else.

Webhook

A reverse API call: instead of you asking a service for news, the service calls you when something happens. You have none, and could not receive one without a backend. Mentioned because it will come up if you ever add "email me when someone registers".

HTTPS and certificates

The s means the connection is encrypted, so nobody between the visitor and the server can read or alter it. It requires a certificate proving the site is who it claims. GitHub Pages issues and renews yours automatically, and your repository has https_enforced: true, so plain HTTP is redirected. Nothing to maintain — but if you add a custom domain you must re-enable it.

2.7  Two words specific to your site

WebGL

A browser API for drawing with the graphics chip (GPU) instead of the normal page-drawing machinery. You reach for it when you need something per-pixel and fast — your portal's torn, refracting edge is computed for every pixel, sixty times a second. The normal CSS engine cannot do that.

Programs that run on the GPU are called shaders and are written in a separate C-like language, GLSL. Yours is 80 lines of text inside js/portal.js:50–128.

Three.js

A popular library that wraps WebGL in friendlier concepts — scenes, cameras, meshes. Your site does not use it, deliberately. Three.js is around 600 KB; your portal has no scene, no camera and no 3D geometry, so it would be a large download to do less. Knowing the name matters because people will assume it is there.

3.2  Why the script order is not negotiable

At the bottom of index.html:

<script src="js/content.js"></script>
<script src="js/portal.js"></script>
<script src="js/app.js"></script>
<script src="js/faq-bot.js"></script>
<script src="js/join.js"></script>
<script src="js/sound.js"></script>

Plain <script> tags run strictly in order, each finishing before the next starts. That is the entire mechanism holding your site together, and it imposes two rules:

  • content.js must come first. It creates the global EDGE object. Every other file reads from it. Move it later and four files fail at once — app.js:14 would log [EDGE] content.js did not load. and stop.
  • portal.js must come before app.js. app.js reads window.EDGE_PORTAL when it wires up the Ask button.

The last three are independent of each other and could be reordered, though there is no reason to.

Why the scripts are at the bottom of the body

A script tag in the <head> blocks the browser from drawing until it has downloaded and run. Putting them last means the visitor sees the page before the JavaScript arrives. Combined with DOMContentLoaded, it also guarantees the HTML elements exist by the time your code looks for them.

3.3  The IIFE pattern, and why every file is wrapped

Every one of your JavaScript files looks like this:

(function () {
  "use strict";
  // ... everything
})();
IIFE — Immediately Invoked Function Expression

A function that is defined and run in the same breath. Anything declared inside is invisible outside. Without it, every variable in every file would share one global namespace, and two files using const log would collide and break each other.

"use strict" turns silent mistakes into loud errors — assigning to an undeclared variable throws instead of quietly creating a global.

Because the files are sealed, anything they deliberately share is attached to window — the browser's global object. Your site has exactly five such handoffs:

3.5  One security decision worth understanding

Every renderer builds text through a helper at js/app.js:20:

function el(tag, className, text) {
  const n = document.createElement(tag);
  if (className) n.className = className;
  if (text != null) n.textContent = text;   // <== the important line
  return n;
}

Setting textContent puts text in as text. The alternative, innerHTML, puts it in as markup — meaning any HTML inside the string becomes real elements.

Why this matters — cross-site scripting

Suppose a player's in-game name were <script>steal()</script>. With textContent, visitors see those characters printed on screen. With innerHTML, the browser would run it, in every visitor's session. That attack is called XSS (cross-site scripting) and it is one of the most common vulnerabilities on the web.

Today all your content is written by you, so the risk is low. The moment any text comes from a database or a form, it becomes real. The rule: never introduce innerHTML into this project. There is currently not a single use of it anywhere in your JavaScript, and that is worth keeping true.

4.6  The CSS trap that has bitten this project twice

When two CSS rules target the same element, the browser picks the winner by specificity — a score based on how precisely the selector names the element, not on which rule appears later.

table.board-table { min-width: 640px; }   /* element + class = stronger */
.board-table     { min-width: 0;     }   /* class only     = weaker  */

The second rule loses even though it comes later and sits inside a phone media query. This is exactly what happened: the leaderboard stayed 640px wide on a 375px phone, and nothing in the browser reported an error. The fix was to match the weight:

table.board-table { min-width: 0; }

The same mistake in the opposite direction broke the logo. A blanket rule img[src*="edge-mark.svg"] was added to control the mark's size; an attribute selector outranks a plain class, so it overrode every component's own height and collapsed the logos.

The habit to build

Before writing a CSS override, find the rule you are trying to beat and copy its selector shape. If the original says table.board-table, your override says table.board-table. Verify in the browser's element inspector, which shows overridden rules struck through — if your new rule appears struck through, you lost on specificity.

6.1  The dimensional portal

Hovering the Ask EDGE button tears an irregular opening in the page, through which a pixel-art world is visible. Clicking commits: the tear grows until that world is all there is. It reverses on the way back.

The central trick

The canvas draws ONLY the destination world

Everywhere outside the tear, it writes alpha 0 — fully transparent. Your actual page is simply still there underneath, showing through.

Nothing about the homepage is captured, copied or re-rendered. That is what keeps the whole effect to a single shape and one small program. A naive implementation would render both worlds and blend them; this renders one world with a hole-shaped mask.

What actually runs on the GPU

Shader

A small program that runs on the graphics chip, once per pixel, in parallel. Yours is written in GLSL and lives as a string in portal.js:50–128. It receives the pixel's position and a handful of values from JavaScript, and returns a colour and an opacity.

Uniform

A value passed from JavaScript to the shader, the same for every pixel in a frame. Yours are: uTex (the video frame), uRes (canvas size), uTexRes (video size), uCenter (where the tear opens), uProgress (0 closed to 1 fully open), uMaxR (radius needed to cover the screen), uTime.

uProgress is the one that matters. JavaScript eases it toward a target; the shader draws whatever that number says. That separation is why a hover can become a click mid-animation without anything restarting.

The shader's core, annotated:

float wob = fbm(vec2(cos(ang), sin(ang)) * 2.4 + uTime * 0.05);
// fbm = fractal noise. Fed the ANGLE around the centre, so the wobble
// varies around the rim rather than across the screen. uTime makes it drift.

float r = uProgress * uMaxR;                      // ideal radius right now
r *= 1.0 + (wob - 0.5) * 0.26 * (1.0 - uProgress * 0.8);
// Distort that radius by up to ±13%, and fade the distortion as it opens —
// so a small tear is ragged and a finished world is not permanently rippling.

float edge = 1.5 + 54.0 * (1.0 - uProgress);      // soft edge, sharpens as it grows
float mask = 1.0 - smoothstep(r - edge, r + edge, dist);
if (mask <= 0.002) discard;                       // outside the tear: draw nothing

Three finishing effects, all fading out via settle as the tear opens:

  • Refraction — near the rim the world is pulled toward the opening, the way something seen through a lens bends at the boundary.
  • Chromatic aberration — red, green and blue are sampled at very slightly different positions along the radius, a fraction of a percent. This is the artefact real lenses produce; it reads as physical rather than digital.
  • Rim light — pow(rim, 7.0) concentrates a thin white line right at the edge. Monochrome, no hue added, because the design system forbids colour in chrome.

You asked for a system that could later carry Players, Updates and Join into their own worlds. That is why portal.js knows nothing about the FAQ. It takes a video and an origin element and does its job.

6.2  The chatbot, honestly described

It is not AI

It is a keyword matcher. It scores what someone typed against the keywords attached to each answer you wrote, and returns the best one. It never generates text. Every word it says is a word you wrote.

This was a deliberate engineering choice, and the reasoning applies to your future projects. A real language model needs an API key. A key shipped inside a public web page can be read by anyone viewing source and used to run up your bill — and there is no way to hide it without a backend. The matcher costs nothing, works offline, never invents an answer, and cannot be abused.

How the matching works

  1. Normalise — lowercase, strip punctuation, split into words.
  2. Drop filler — about 80 words carrying no meaning. Question words (what, how, when) are in the filler list deliberately: when they counted as keywords, "what is the weather today" matched "What is EDGE?" purely on the word "what".
  3. Stem — a crude suffix-stripper so "years" matches "year" and "casting" matches "cast". Applied to both sides so they meet in the middle.
  4. Score — +2 per word matching a keyword, +1 per word appearing in the question text, +3 if the typed text is nearly the whole question.
  5. Normalise by length — divide by the square root of the word count.
  6. Compare to the confidence bar (1.4). Above it, answer. Below it, say so honestly and offer the nearest questions instead of guessing.
Why square root, and why this is a good lesson

Dividing by the full word count punished natural phrasing far too hard: "i want to sign up" scored below a bare "join", because three of its words carried no signal. The square root still stops a long rambling question from beating a short precise one, but much more gently.

The general lesson: when a scoring system misbehaves, the fix is usually to change how you normalise, not to add special cases.

The security model — the most important thing in your project

Once you connect Supabase, your anon key sits in content.js where anyone can read it. That is intended. What protects the data is not secrecy but a database rule.

Row Level Security (RLS)

A Postgres feature letting the database decide, per row and per operation, who may do what. Supabase publishes every table over a public REST API, so RLS is the only thing standing between your anon key and your data.

supabase-schema.sql creates exactly one policy:

create policy "public can register"
  on public.registrations
  for insert          -- INSERT only
  to anon             -- for anonymous visitors
  with check (true);

And deliberately creates no others. No select policy means reads are denied by default. No update policy means nobody can alter a submission. No delete policy means nobody can wipe your registrations. You read the data while signed in to the Supabase dashboard, which runs as the service role and bypasses RLS.

Get this wrong and you leak every student's phone number

Without that policy — or with a select policy added carelessly — anyone could take the key from your page and download every name, register number, phone number and email address you have collected.

The schema file ends with two curl commands to verify it. The insert must succeed; the select must come back as an empty list []. Run both before the site handles a single real registration. If the select returns rows, stop and fix it.

The database also re-validates everything the browser checked — phone shape, email shape, and that a roster application has a game and an in-game name. This is not redundancy. Client-side validation is a convenience for the person filling the form; it is not a guarantee, because anyone can POST directly at the API and skip your JavaScript entirely. The database constraints are the actual protection.

A program on your computer that records snapshots of your project. Each snapshot is a commit, with a message saying what changed and why. Git works entirely offline. It is your undo history, at project scale, forever.

GitHub

A website that stores a copy of your git repository in the cloud. It is your backup, your collaboration point, and — for you — your deployment mechanism. Your account is potatoesareathing; your repository is edge-website.

GitHub Pages

A feature of GitHub that takes whatever is committed on a chosen branch and serves it as a website. Yours is configured to serve the main branch from the root folder. It runs no code — it only hands out files.

Why the subpath works at all

Because every asset path in your site is relative — css/styles.css, not /css/styles.css. A leading slash means "from the domain root", which would resolve to github.io/css/styles.css and 404.

Rule: never start an asset path with a slash in this project. It will work in local preview and break in production, which is the worst kind of bug.

Caching

GitHub Pages sets its own cache headers and you cannot configure them. The rules in netlify.toml do nothing. In practice Pages serves assets with a ten-minute cache, which is short enough that stale files are rarely a problem — but when you do replace an image and still see the old one, add or bump a version marker:

<img src="assets/edge-mark.svg?v=3">   <!-- was ?v=2 -->

The browser treats a different query string as a different file. This is why every logo reference in your HTML already carries ?v=2.

One Pages-specific requirement

GitHub Pages runs files through Jekyll, a site generator, which ignores any file or folder whose name starts with an underscore. Your project has none, so it works. If you ever add _drafts/ or _assets/, those files will silently not publish. The fix is an empty file named .nojekyll at the repository root.

Part 12Troubleshooting: how to find a fault yourself

The instinct when something breaks is to describe the symptom to an AI and ask for a fix. That works until it doesn't, and it never teaches you anything. This part teaches the alternative: locate the fault before you try to fix it.

12.1  The discipline

Debugging is not guessing repeatedly. It is narrowing a search space. Every step should eliminate possibilities, and you should be able to say what you learned from each one.

  1. Reproduce it. Exactly what did you click, on what device, in what browser? A bug you cannot reproduce is one you cannot verify you fixed.
  2. Locate the layer. Which of the seven layers in 12.2 is it in? Most of your time should go here.
  3. Read the error. Error messages are precise. Beginners skim them; the message almost always names the file and the line.
  4. Form one hypothesis and test it. One at a time, or you will not know which change worked.
  5. Fix, verify, and write down what it was. A bug that recurs and is re-diagnosed from scratch has cost you twice.

12.5  Diagnosing a CSS problem properly

CSS problems feel mysterious because nothing errors. The method removes the mystery:

  1. Right-click the misbehaving element → Inspect.
  2. The Styles panel lists every rule affecting it, strongest first.
  3. Find the property that is wrong. If your intended rule appears struck through, it lost on specificity — the winning rule is above it. Match its selector shape (4.6).
  4. If the property is not listed at all, your selector does not match. Check for a typo or a missing parent class.
  5. Edit the value directly in the panel to confirm your fix works, then make the same change in styles.css.
  6. Use the Computed tab to see the final value of everything, and the box-model diagram to see actual padding, border and margin.

12.6  The most likely error you will ever cause

A syntax error in content.js. It empties the entire site, which is alarming and completely recoverable.

Uncaught SyntaxError: Unexpected token '}'    content.js:241

Almost always one of three things:

  • A missing comma between two entries in an array.
  • An unescaped apostrophe inside a single-quoted string: 'don't' ends the string early. Use double quotes: "don't".
  • A missing closing bracket } or ].

The reported line number is where the parser noticed, which is often a line or two after the real mistake. Look just above it.

git diff js/content.js     # see exactly what you changed
git checkout -- js/content.js   # or: throw it all away and start again

12.7  Deciding whether it is your fault

TestWhat it tells you
Does it break in a private/incognito window? No → it is a cache or an extension on your machine, not a real bug.
Does it break in a different browser? No → a browser-specific issue, usually a feature one browser lacks.
Did it work at the last commit? git stash, reload, git stash pop. If the old version works, your change caused it — and git diff shows you exactly what you did.
Is the file reaching the browser at all? Network tab. A 404 is a path problem, not a code problem.
Is GitHub itself down? githubstatus.com. Rare, but it happens, and there is nothing to fix.

12.8  When you do ask for help

Asking well is a skill, and it is the same skill whether you are asking a person, a forum or an AI. Include:

  • What you expected and what happened instead.
  • The exact error text, copied, not described.
  • Which layer you have already ruled out, and how.
  • The relevant code, not the whole file.
  • What you already tried.

That list is not politeness. Writing it out solves a surprising share of problems before you finish — the act of stating precisely what you expected is often where the wrong assumption reveals itself.

The rule that makes you independent

Never apply a fix you do not understand. If something is suggested and you cannot say why it works, ask for the reasoning before applying it. A site held together by changes you cannot explain is a site you cannot maintain — and you will have simply traded one dependency for another.

Part 13Things to understand before your next website

Concepts this project already taught you, made explicit so they transfer. Each one is stated as a working principle, not a definition, with the evidence from your own code.

13.1  Separation of concerns

Your site keeps content, appearance and behaviour in separate files. That is why you can update the leaderboard without touching a line of logic, and why the whole design was converted from colour to monochrome without breaking a single feature.

The test: to change one thing, how many files must you open? If a content change requires editing logic, the separation has failed.

13.2  One source of truth

Every piece of content lives in exactly one place. The leaderboard is in content.js and nowhere else. If the same fact lived in two files, they would eventually disagree, and the bug would be invisible until someone noticed the site contradicting itself.

Apply it everywhere: colours as CSS variables, not repeated hex codes. Dates in one format. One list of tab names (TABS in app.js) driving the router.

13.3  Components are just functions that build things

You do not need React to have components. postCard(p) takes data and returns a finished DOM node. Called ten times, you get ten cards. That is the idea; frameworks add lifecycle management and automatic updates on top of it.

The lesson: understand the pattern before you adopt the tool. People who learn React first often cannot explain what it is doing for them.

13.4  State is where bugs live

Your site has very little state, and it is deliberately confined: the current tab, the join path, the portal's progress. Each lives in one place with one owner.

The rule that follows: never store the same truth twice. The portal knows its progress; the CSS reads it. If both JavaScript and CSS each tracked "is the portal open", they would drift apart, and the resulting bug would be maddening.

13.5  Design for failure, because it will

This is the most valuable thing in your codebase. Count the fallbacks:

  • No WebGL → the world still opens, plainly.
  • Video will not load → the poster image stands in.
  • Reduced motion → every effect is off and nothing is downloaded.
  • Scroll observer never fires → a two-second timer shows everything anyway.
  • Portal stalls → a 2.6-second timer lands it rather than freezing part-open.
  • Network drops mid-submission → the row is queued and retried.
  • Instagram post URL missing → the card is skipped silently.
  • Google Fonts unreachable → fallback fonts.

The principle: for every feature, ask what happens when it fails, and make that outcome acceptable rather than catastrophic. A site that degrades is a site that keeps working for the person on the worst phone and the weakest connection — which, on a university campus, is a lot of people.

13.6  Never trust the client

Your form validates carefully in the browser and the database re-validates everything. That is not redundancy. Anyone can bypass your JavaScript entirely and POST straight at the API. Browser validation is a courtesy to the person filling the form; the database constraint is the guarantee.

Generalise it: any check that matters must happen somewhere the user cannot reach.

13.7  Security is about permissions, not secrecy

Your Supabase key is published and that is correct. What protects the data is a rule saying anonymous visitors may insert and may do nothing else.

The general form: assume anything in the frontend is public, because it is. Grant the minimum permission that makes the feature work, and nothing more. Your database has no select policy, no update policy and no delete policy — not because they were forgotten, but because each would be an unnecessary permission.

13.8  Every dependency is a liability

Your site has zero and will run untouched for years. A typical beginner project has hundreds, each an opportunity for breakage, abandonment or a security advisory.

The question to ask before adding anything: what does this do that thirty lines of my own code could not? Sometimes the honest answer justifies it. Often it does not.

13.9  Version control is not backup — it is time travel

Git lets you see exactly what you changed before committing, undo a specific change from weeks ago, and recover a file you deleted. Your fifteen commits each carry a message explaining why, which is how you will understand your own decisions in a year.

Habits worth keeping: commit small and often; write messages that explain the reasoning, not the diff; never commit something you have not looked at with git diff.

13.10  Performance is mostly about what you do not send

Your page is around 200 KB before the video is conditionally attached. The videos are 97% of the total, which is exactly why they are gated behind three checks.

The lesson: the fastest asset is the one never downloaded. Before optimising code, look at what you are shipping at all. One unoptimised photograph can outweigh your entire codebase.

13.11  Accessibility is not an extra

Your ARIA tabs, keyboard navigation, form labels and reduced-motion support cost very little and were built in from the start. Retrofitting them would have been a rewrite.

The cheapest rules: use real <button> elements; give every image an alt; label every input; make sure everything is reachable by Tab. That is most of the value for very little effort.

13.12  Technical debt, and the honest kind

Debt is a shortcut you take deliberately, knowing you will pay interest. Your project has some and it is documented, which is what makes it debt rather than rot: netlify.toml left in place, the register-number rule left permissive, the placeholder content, the portal's single-instance state.

The distinction: a known shortcut with a note is manageable. An unknown one is a trap waiting for someone who has forgotten. Write the note.

13.13  Write down why, not what

Your code comments explain reasoning: why the video src is attached in JavaScript, why the scoring divides by a square root, why rAF is not used for the audio ramp. The code already says what it does; only a comment can say why.

DESIGN.md goes further and records mistakes that were made and corrected. That is unusual and genuinely valuable — it is what stops the same mistake being made twice.
